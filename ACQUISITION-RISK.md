# Terms-of-service risk assessment — live price acquisition

**Task 7.9 · Req 8.8 · Assessed 10 August 2026**

This gates Task 7.5 (per-retailer acquisition) and Task 11.4 (live price
acquisition). It is an engineering risk assessment, not legal advice. Where it
records a legal position it records the *source* alongside it, so a reader can
check the reasoning rather than trust the conclusion.

The assessment reaches a **split decision** — see §7. The short form: the
binding constraints are not the ones this kind of project usually worries
about, and the one that bites hardest sits on our output rather than on our
fetching.

---

## 1. Scope

"Live acquisition" means obtaining prices from Pak'nSave, New World
(Foodstuffs North Island / South Island) and Woolworths New Zealand, in place
of the seed dataset generated by `scripts/generate_fixtures.py`.

Assessed: each retailer's published terms and `robots.txt`; the New Zealand
statutes that bear on automated collection and on republishing prices; the
regulatory posture of the Commerce Commission; and the observed behaviour of
comparable services already operating in this market.

Not assessed: contractual exposure arising from an account or API credential,
because none is held. If a retailer relationship is ever established, that
introduces terms this document does not cover and it must be re-run.

## 3. Findings by source

### 3.1 Foodstuffs — Pak'nSave and New World

The website terms contain **no prohibition on automated access**. No robot,
spider, crawler, scraping, data-mining or systematic-retrieval clause was
found. This is unusual and worth stating plainly, because the assumption
running into this task was that such a clause existed.

What they do contain is an intellectual property clause: material on the site
"may be protected by copyright, trade mark, patent or other protections, even
if it is not stated to be", and you are "not permitted to do anything which
infringes these intellectual property rights (such as copying, modifying or
reposting content) unless you have express permission from us."

That constrains *what we republish*, not *how we fetch*. It is a copyright
restatement, and §4.3 deals with it.

**`robots.txt` is the real finding.** Both Foodstuffs sites disallow, for all
user-agents, the search paths:

```
Disallow: /search
Disallow: /Search
Disallow: /shop/search
Disallow: /shop/Search
```

The search endpoint is precisely where price data lives, and precisely what a
price acquirer would call. Both sites publish e-commerce product sitemaps and
leave product pages themselves crawlable — so the expressed preference is not
"stay out", it is "traverse the catalogue we published, do not drive the
search box".

That is a usable distinction, and it happens to be the technically better
approach anyway: a sitemap traversal is enumerable, cacheable and stable,
where a search-endpoint crawl is neither.

`robots.txt` is not contractually binding on its own. It is, however, the
clearest available statement of the operator's wishes, and disregarding it is
the fact that converts a defensible activity into an indefensible one in every
head of liability below. Treat it as binding.

### 3.2 Woolworths New Zealand

Terms not retrievable — see §2. The Australian parent's site terms prohibit
use of "any robot, spider, site search and retrieval application or other
mechanism to retrieve or index any portion of the Site". Whether the NZ entity
carries the same clause is **unconfirmed and must not be assumed either way**.

Given an unretrievable `robots.txt`, apparent bot mitigation, and a parent
with an explicit prohibition, Woolworths NZ is the highest-risk of the three
and is treated as prohibited until a human confirms otherwise.

## 4. Exposure under New Zealand law

There is no dedicated scraping statute. Exposure is assembled from five
sources, and they are **not equally weighted** — the ranking in §6 is the
substantive output of this section.

### 4.1 Contract — breach of terms of use

Breach of website terms is a contractual matter, not a criminal one. Ordinary
consequence is a cease-and-desist; escalation beyond that requires the
operator to establish loss.

Enforceability against a party that never registered an account is weaker than
it looks — browsewrap terms bind poorly without assent. This is not a defence
to rely on, but it does place ToS breach below where instinct puts it.

### 4.2 Crimes Act 1961, s249 — accessing a computer system for dishonest gain

**This is the one people fear and it is the one least likely to apply.**
s249 requires *dishonest* access to obtain a benefit. Accessing a website
intended for public use and collecting data from it — even where not permitted
by the terms — is using a public system for a non-permitted purpose, which is
not the same thing as dishonest access.

The risk becomes real at a bright line: **circumventing a technical control.**
Defeating bot mitigation, forging credentials, using an undocumented internal
endpoint the site does not expose to its own public interface, or exploiting a
vulnerability moves the conduct across it.

This is why §3.2 matters so much. Woolworths NZ appears to run bot mitigation.
Working around it would not be a terms problem — it would be the only path in
this entire assessment with criminal exposure attached. **Do not.**

### 4.3 Copyright Act 1994

Copyright protects expression, not facts. **A price is a fact.** "Wattie's
Baked Beans 420g" is close to a fact. Product photography, marketing
descriptions, curated recipe content and the selection-and-arrangement of a
catalogue are expression, and are protected.

This maps cleanly onto our design and mostly resolves itself:

- We store a normalised product key, a name, a size, a retailer and a
  `Decimal` price. That is the factual layer.
- We do **not** store or serve retailer images, marketing copy or descriptions
  — and there is no reason to start.
- Our output cites a retailer and a price. It does not reproduce their page.

The residual risk is a *substantial-part* argument over the catalogue as a
compilation — reproducing the whole product set rather than answering queries
against it. Storing only the subset needed to answer a query, rather than
mirroring the catalogue wholesale, keeps this small.

### 4.4 Privacy Act 2020

**Not engaged.** Prices are not personal information, and Req 8.7 already
requires that the price store retain none. Nothing in live acquisition changes
that, provided acquisition does not start collecting reviewer names, store
staff details or anything else attached to a person. It must not.

This is a requirement we already satisfy — it is listed so that a future change
which starts ingesting reviews is understood to reopen it.

### 4.5 Fair Trading Act 1986

**This is the binding constraint, and it attaches to our output rather than
our collection.** Liability for a misleading price comparison sits with the
party publishing the comparison — us — not with the retailer whose price was
stale, and not with the act of fetching.

Scraped price data is incomplete, time-lagged and context-dependent: store-level
price variation, club or loyalty pricing, multi-buy conditions, and promotions
that expire between capture and display. A confident "cheapest at X" derived
from a four-hour-old capture at a different store is capable of misleading a
consumer, and that is the conduct the Act reaches.

This is the same failure mode the project already treats as a first-class
invariant. "Honest failure over plausible answers" and the rule that no price
may originate from model generation both exist because a confidently wrong
price is worse than no price. **The Fair Trading Act is that invariant with a
statute behind it.** Live acquisition does not introduce a new discipline; it
raises the cost of abandoning the one already in place.

Concretely, it means the capture date recorded under Req 8.4 is not
bookkeeping — it is the evidential basis for every comparison we publish, and
it must be surfaced to the user, not merely stored.

## 5. Regulatory context

The Commerce Commission regulates this sector under the Grocery Industry
Competition Act 2023. Its March 2022 market study found competition working
poorly for consumers and made 14 recommendations, 12 accepted by government,
including mandatory unit pricing (in force 31 August 2023) and pressure on the
major retailers to make pricing and promotional practices simple and easy to
understand.

The direction of regulatory travel is toward price transparency, and a
consumer price-comparison tool runs with that current rather than against it.
A retailer moving aggressively against such a tool would be doing so in front
of a regulator that has already found against its market conduct.

**This is a mitigating factor, not a defence.** It affects the *likelihood* of
enforcement, not whether conduct is lawful. It does not license anything in §4.

One claim could not be verified: that the market study specifically recommends
supermarkets cooperate with price comparison services. It is repeated in press
coverage, attributed to a comparison-app developer rather than to the report
text, and it did not survive a direct check against Commission sources. **It is
not relied on here.** If it is real it strengthens §7's permission route, and
it is worth ten minutes with the final report to confirm.

## 6. Risk, ranked

Ranked by expected cost — likelihood against consequence. The ordering is the
point; it is close to the inverse of the intuitive one.

1. **Fair Trading Act — misleading comparison.** Highest. Near-certain to
   arise the moment stale or store-mismatched prices are published with
   confidence, entirely self-inflicted, and mitigated by engineering we
   already believe in.
2. **Breach of terms / `robots.txt` disregard.** Moderate likelihood, low
   immediate consequence (cease-and-desist), but it is the fact that
   aggravates everything else. Cheap to avoid.
3. **Copyright — compilation.** Low, and lower still if we store facts and
   query subsets rather than mirroring catalogues.
4. **Crimes Act s249.** Very low *unless* a technical control is circumvented,
   at which point it is the most serious item on the list. Binary, and under
   our control.
5. **Privacy Act 2020.** Not engaged; already precluded by Req 8.7.

## 7. Decision

**Task 7.5 is unblocked. Task 11.4 remains gated.**

The original note recorded that 7.9 gates 7.5 — "legal assessment precedes
implementation, not the reverse". That sequencing was right, and having run it,
the two halves separate:

**7.5 may proceed.** It is the per-retailer acquisition *structure* —
independent adapters, isolated failure, one retailer's outage not taking the
others down (Req 8.5). Built against fixtures and recorded responses, it sends
no production traffic and carries none of the exposure in §4. Blocking it
gains nothing, and the structure is the part that needs to exist before any
question about live sources can be answered concretely. Same argument as the
protocol boundaries in `src/retrieval/base.py`: build the seam offline, decide
what sits behind it separately.

**11.4 stays gated, but on named conditions instead of an open question.**
The conditions are §8. This is the actual value delivered by this task — the
gate was previously "risk unassessed", which is unfalsifiable and therefore
never clears.

**The recommended route to 11.4 is written permission, not careful scraping.**
Given §5, an approach to Foodstuffs and Woolworths NZ explaining a
non-commercial workshop project has a materially better chance than it would
in most markets, and permission dissolves items 1–4 of §6 at once. It costs an
email. It should be sent before any engineering effort is spent on §8's
controls, because a yes makes most of them unnecessary and a no is itself the
answer to whether 11.4 should happen at all.

## 8. Conditions on any live acquisition

Every one of these must hold before Task 11.4 is considered started. They are
written as acceptance criteria so the rebuild inherits them.

> **Engineering design that implements these conditions:**
> [`docs/LIVE-ACQUISITION-DESIGN.md`](docs/LIVE-ACQUISITION-DESIGN.md). It is
> **design-only and parked** — it maps each condition below to a named control
> in the sitemap-traversal adapter that would sit behind the existing
> `ingestion/sources.py` seam, and it recommends sending the §7 permission email
> *before* any engineering. It does not unblock this gate; it is the plan for
> when the gate clears.

**Access**

1. The three unretrieved sources in §2 have been read by a human and the table
   completed.
2. `robots.txt` is honoured for every retailer, re-fetched and re-checked on
   each run rather than transcribed once into code. Foodstuffs search paths
   are not requested.
3. Catalogue traversal uses the published product sitemaps, not the search
   endpoint.
4. **No technical control is circumvented, ever, for any reason.** Not bot
   mitigation, not rate limiting, not an undocumented internal endpoint. If a
   retailer blocks us, that is the answer, and the correct response is to ask
   or to stop. This is the §4.2 bright line and it is not a judgement call.
5. A descriptive `User-Agent` identifying the project with a contact address.
   Anonymity is not available to a defensible collector.
6. Conservative rate limiting, well below anything that could degrade service,
   with backoff on any error and a hard stop on repeated failure. Req 8.4's
   schedule is a daily refresh, not a continuous crawl.

**Storage**

7. Facts only — key, name, size, retailer, `Decimal` price, capture date. No
   images, no marketing copy, no descriptions, no reviews, no personal
   information (Req 8.7).
8. Store the subset required to answer queries, not a mirror of the catalogue.

**Output — the §4.5 controls, and the ones that matter most**

9. Capture date is surfaced to the user with every comparison, not merely
   stored. A price the user cannot date is a price they cannot evaluate.
10. Prices older than a defined staleness threshold are not presented as
    current — they take the `no_data` path (Req 4.1–4.2), which already exists
    and already returns a contract-valid success.
11. Store-level scope is stated. If a captured price is not store-specific,
    the comparison must not imply that it is.
12. Conditional pricing — club, loyalty, multi-buy, promotional — is either
    represented faithfully or excluded. It is never flattened into a headline
    price.
13. No superlative claim ("cheapest", "best price") is made beyond what the
    retrieved data supports at the recorded capture time.

Conditions 9–13 are Fair Trading Act compliance expressed as engineering. They
are also, almost exactly, the existing invariants applied to a new data source
— which is the reassuring part of this assessment.

## 9. Review triggers

Re-run this assessment if any of the following changes:

- A retailer's terms or `robots.txt` change, or a retailer makes contact.
- The project acquires an account, API key or commercial relationship with any
  retailer — §1 explicitly does not cover that.
- The output moves from a workshop context to public or commercial deployment;
  §5's mitigating posture and §4.1's browsewrap weakness both shift.
- Acquisition begins collecting anything beyond the §8.7 field list,
  particularly anything attached to a person.
- The Commerce Commission's grocery-sector position materially changes.
